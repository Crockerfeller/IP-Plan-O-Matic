IPv6 subnetting: /48, /56, /64 and nibbles

In IPv6 you stop counting hosts. Every LAN gets a /64, and planning is about how many /64s each site gets.

Standard sizes

PrefixTypical use/64 networks inside
/32An ISP or large organization allocation4,294,967,296
/48One site or campus65,536
/56A small site, branch or home connection256
/60A small home or lab allocation16
/64One LAN or VLAN (SLAAC needs exactly /64)1
/127A point-to-point link between routers (RFC 6164)—
/128A single address, such as a loopback—

Split on nibbles

Each hex digit in an IPv6 address is 4 bits, a “nibble”. Splitting on 4-bit boundaries (/48 → /52 → /56 → /60 → /64) keeps every prefix readable: the subnets of 2001:db8:abcd::/48 at /52 are 2001:db8:abcd:0000::/52 through 2001:db8:abcd:f000::/52, one hex digit each. It also keeps reverse DNS clean, because each ip6.arpa label is one nibble.

A /48 holds 16 /52s, 256 /56s and 65,536 /64s. A common plan gives each building or region a /52 or /56 and each VLAN a /64.

Point-to-point links

Use a /127 for router-to-router links (RFC 6164), often taken from a /64 reserved per link so the numbering stays simple.

Unique local addresses (ULA)

For private IPv6, RFC 4193 reserves fc00::/7; in practice you use fd00::/8 plus a random 40-bit Global ID, giving you your own /48. Randomness matters: two companies that both pick fd00::/48 will clash if they ever connect.

Reverse DNS

Reverse zones live under ip6.arpa, one label per nibble in reverse. 2001:db8:abcd::/48 becomes d.c.b.a.8.b.d.0.1.0.0.2.ip6.arpa. Prefixes that aren’t on a nibble boundary span several zones.

Split a /48 into nibble-aligned prefixes, give each a name and VLAN, or auto-plan “Sites, /56, 12” in one go.
